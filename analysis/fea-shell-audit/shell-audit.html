How this was checked

Reading a hand-derived tangent stiffness does not tell you whether it is right. Differentiating it does.

Every claim in this report rests on one of four kinds of executable check, run against the uploaded source with no modification to it:

CheckWhat it settlesHow a wrong belief shows up

Scripts for all four are listed in section 06 and are committed alongside this report.

One measurement decides most of what follows. On the code's own cantilever mesh, driven by the code's own solver to 30 % of full load, the assembled internal force differs from the gradient of the assembled strain energy by 16.3 %. A Newton solve that converges to ||R|| = 5.8×10-10 is therefore converging to the solution of a slightly different problem than the one the element's kinematics define.

01

Formulation defects

Nine findings, ordered by what they cost. The first three share one root cause and one fix.

F1

The bending B-matrix is built from the reference parametrisation while the strain it differentiates uses the current one

Wrong answer
shell_element.py:76  ·  shell_patch_kinematics.py:134

shell_patch_kinematics forms the current curvature as c_curr = S @ Tinv_curr @ z_curr, using the quadratic-fit matrix evaluated on the deformed patch. shell_element_routine then hands Tinv = kin['Tinv_ref'] to B1_matrix and B_b_matrix, together with xi_curr, eta_curr and z_curr. The resulting operator is a hybrid that differentiates neither the frozen-reference curvature nor the convected one.

At zero displacement the two coincide, so a linear analysis is unaffected. The error grows with the total displacement measured from the reference state, which is exactly the regime the large-deflection benchmarks live in.

Fix. Use kin['Tinv_curr'] at shell_element.py:76, or change shell_patch_kinematics.py:134 to build c_curr from Tinv_ref. The two choices are different elements; they are not both defensible at once. Substituting Tinv_curr alone drops the residual error by a factor of 24 at 10 % displacement, and what remains is F2.

F2

H1 omits the rotation of the contravariant basis with the surface normal

Wrong answer
bending_matrices.py:33–80 (H1_matrix)

The patch coordinate of neighbour i is ξi = ξi0 + bi·a1, so its variation is δbi·a1 + bi·δa1. Decomposing δaα in the local frame gives three terms: two tangential ones, which the code carries as the k and -l coefficients, and one normal one, −zi(aα·δn), which is absent.

That term is proportional to zi, the out-of-plane offset of the neighbour from the plane of the primary triangle. It vanishes identically on a flat undeformed patch, which is why the omission survives a flat-plate check. On the cantilever mesh at 30 % load, |zi| reaches 21 % of the element size.

flat patch, u = 10-1, as coded5.7e-02
same, missing term restored5.0e-10

Fix. Add to each row of H1, for the columns belonging to δb2 and δb3, the contribution +zi(aα P M1)/L and +zi(aα P M2)/L respectively, with P = I − n⊗n, L = |a1×a2| and Mα the existing cross_matrix(aα). The same projector already appears in B2_matrix, which linearises zi = bi·n correctly.

Part of this follows from F1 and F2, since K1, K2 and K3 are built from the same B1, H1 and Tinv. Both halves have to move together.

The practical signature is in the iteration counts. On the cantilever the shipped code needs 8, 15, 17, 16 and 23 Newton iterations for the first five load steps of a smooth problem where a consistent tangent would need three or four, and the residual falls linearly rather than quadratically.

F5

The clamped-edge ghost transformation drops its dependence on the second edge node, and the disabled line has the wrong sign

Latent
bending_matrices.py:211–222 (apply_bending_edge_transformation)

For a 'fixed' edge the ghost sits at rJ = rI + 2(nI0·(rF−rI))nI0 (ghost.py:107), whose exact linearisation has two blocks: ∂rJ/∂rI = I − 2n⊗n and ∂rJ/∂rF = +2n⊗n. The code writes the first as RI and applies it. The second is commented out at line 222, and the commented definition at line 212 reads RF = -2.0 * np.outer(N_I0, N_I0), which is the negative of the correct block.

Y[ghost rows] against a central difference of the ghost position rule
'free' edge, QI / QP / QF2.0e-09
'fixed' edge, RI only7.6e-01
the free-edge branch is an exact linearisation; the fixed branch is not

The omission is harmless in every benchmark in the archive, because those clamped edges restrain all three translations at both edge nodes, so δrF = 0. It becomes wrong the moment a 'fixed' rotational edge sits on nodes that can move: a symmetry plane, a partially restrained support, or a displacement-controlled edge. It also perturbs the reported reactions, which are read from F_int at prescribed DOFs in maindriver_roof.py:327.

Fix. Restore the block with the correct sign: Y[3*ghost:3*ghost+3, 3*F:3*F+3] = 2.0 * np.outer(N_I0, N_I0). No second-derivative term is needed, because N_I0 is a reference quantity and the map is linear in the current coordinates.

F7

The crease penalty is written on the cosine of the fold angle, so its stiffness varies by two orders of magnitude with the fold

Modelling
constraint_penalty.py:64–73

The stored energy is ½κ(cosθ0 − cosθ)². Expanding about θ0 gives ½κsin²θ0(θ−θ0)², so the effective rotational stiffness of the hinge is κsin²θ0, not κ. Creases are created in mesh.py:106 whenever the dihedral angle exceeds angle_tol_deg = 5, so sin²θ0 ranges from 0.0076 at a 5° fold to 1.0 at 90°. A single kappa = 100 therefore represents a hinge that is up to 130 times stiffer on a box corner than on a shallow crease.

Two further consequences follow from using the cosine. It is even in the fold angle, so the constraint cannot distinguish +θ from −θ and a crease can invert through the flat state at no energy cost. And the leading term vanishes at θ0 = 0 and θ0 = π, where the penalty becomes quartic and effectively free.

Fix. Penalise the angle itself, ½κ(θ−θ0)² with θ = atan2((n1×n2)·e, n1·n2) along the hinge axis e, which is signed and has uniform stiffness. Scale κ by the hinge length so the result is mesh independent; as written, refining the mesh multiplies the total hinge stiffness by the number of edges along the crease.

F8

The free-edge ghost condition depends on how the quads were split, and on one common triangulation the solution converges to the wrong answer

Wrong answer
ghost.py:3–7 with bending_matrices.py:215–218; shape_functions.py basis

A free edge is treated by mirroring the opposite vertex about the edge, in the current element plane, so the ghost carries z = 0 by construction. Substituting that into the quadratic fit z = v1(ξ²−ξ) + v2(η²−η) + v3ξη annihilates whichever coefficient the mirror's parametric position happens to select. Which coefficient that is depends on the mirror's (ξ,η), and therefore on the shape and orientation of the boundary triangle rather than on the edge normal.

The consequence is measurable. A simply supported square plate under uniform pressure, solved linearly, converges cleanly on a structured right-triangle mesh and converges to a value 33.6 % too large on a union-jack mesh, which is the same grid with alternating diagonals:

Mesh8×816×1624×2432×32

union-jack alternating, error in wmax+36.75 %+34.21 %+33.76 %+33.61 %
Reference: wmax = 0.00406 qL4/D (Timoshenko and Woinowsky-Krieger). Load kept at 10-6 so the response is linear.

The union-jack solution is smooth, not oscillatory: its second difference along the centre line decays as h² exactly like the structured one, so this is not a spurious mode. The interior operator is not at fault either. A manufactured-solution test that imposes an exact deflection field and compares the assembled discrete bending energy against the Kirchhoff energy gives, on interior elements and across six different imposed fields, ratios to exact between 0.9949 and 1.0002 at 32×32 on both topologies, converging as h². Including the boundary ring the full-domain ratio is 0.9746 at 8×8 and 0.9993 at 48×48, again identical for the two topologies. The interior is consistent; the boundary treatment is what does not converge.

Practical reading: results from this element are only trustworthy on meshes whose boundary triangles all present the same local edge to the free boundary. Every mesh generator in the archive happens to satisfy that. An imported Abaqus mesh through read_abaqus_inp need not, and nothing in the code checks or warns.

Fix. Replace the geometric mirror with the condition it is standing in for. For a free edge the requirement is Mnn = 0, that is κnn + νκss = 0 with n the edge normal. Impose that as an explicit linear constraint on (v1, v2, v3) expressed in the edge frame, so the condition is a property of the edge and not of the local node numbering.

The four-component Voigt convention is a good decision

Carrying [E11, E22, E12, E21] with both shear slots rather than an engineering shear strain, and building H over the matching index pairs at shell_element.py:19, makes the double contraction HαβγδEγδ come out right with no factor-of-two bookkeeping. It is one of the places this code is cleaner than most textbook implementations.

The quadrature is not the problem

Membrane strain is constant across the patch and the fitted curvature is one value per patch, so multiplying by the reference area is exact for the element's own fields. The three-point rule in gauss_quadrature.py is only used by the traction and load-stiffness routines, and there it produces the correct area/3 lumping.

Sign convention: unconventional, and consistent

Eb = c_ref − c_curr at shell_patch_kinematics.py:138 is the negative of the usual change of curvature, and B_b carries the matching sign. The two cancel in BTDε and in BTDB, and the geometric terms are built from the matching stress resultant. This looked like a bug and is not one.

The hinged cylindrical panel benchmark works

Displacement control on the crown, with the backtracking line search in maindriver_roof.py, tracks the full snap-through path in 5 to 6 Newton iterations per step on a 10×10 mesh: limit load 2169 N against a reference 2229 N, and minimum 532 N against 527 N. This is the code at its best.

Performance: where the time goes and what it could be

The code is not slow because the mathematics is expensive. It is slow because the mathematics is executed one three-by-three block at a time through the Python interpreter.

If the goal is to finish the thesis

Take configuration B. It is a day of work, changes no mathematics, and is verifiable by asserting that the new element returns bitwise the same arrays as the old one. It removes the residual-assembly waste (F11) and the dense-matrix ceiling, which is what currently stops the fine slit mesh from running at all.

If the goal is a code that can be trusted

Take configuration C. Writing the element as a scalar energy function and letting jax.grad and jax.jacfwd produce the force and tangent deletes bending_matrices.py in its entirety, which is 719 of the 6977 lines and the location of three of the four highest-severity findings. The energy fits in about 40 lines.

A C++ rewrite is the right answer only if the target is a mesh size that JAX on GPU cannot reach, or an embedded or coupled-solver deployment. For the meshes in this archive it costs weeks and buys perhaps a further factor of two over configuration C, since at 28 µs per element the sparse factorisation is already comparable to assembly.
